How this simulation works

Each station processes one item at a time. Three workers is the baseline staffing level; changing staffing proportionally changes all station capacities. Downtime is modeled as a reduction in effective processing rate, not as random failures. Finished items wait if the next buffer is full. When station capacities are close, the line is described as balanced rather than warning about a bottleneck. A station with lower capacity can become a bottleneck as queues build; actual throughput can be lower during startup or blocking. Workers are modeled as a proportional capacity multiplier across all stations, a teaching simplification rather than a labor-allocation model.

Ten real seconds represent one simulated minute, giving learners more time to observe. Projected throughput estimates the steady-state production rate from the slowest effective station capacity under the current settings. It updates immediately when station speeds, staffing, or average downtime change, and does not fluctuate with individual completions. This idealized capacity estimate assumes a continuously supplied line and does not include startup losses, transient blocking, or variability; actual completed units may differ. This is an instructional model, not a validated industrial forecast.

Research sources and model limitations

This teaching model draws on manufacturing-systems education and lean process guidance. It illustrates single-piece flow, finite intermediate buffers, blocking, work in progress, and throughput. It does not model defects, rework, machine-specific worker assignments, transport times, or random equipment failures. Downtime is a fixed average rate reduction.
